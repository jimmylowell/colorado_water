How every number is graded

Each displayed value carries a method — how much to trust it, and where it came from:

  • measured A published or telemetered reading from the operator of the dam or gage (USGS, Colorado DWR, USBR, Denver Water). Taken as-is.
  • derived Arithmetic on measured values: percent of normal = a current reading ÷ that same station's median for this week of the year. The medians are baked once from each station's own long record (see the ledger below), not guessed.
  • estimated No same-day public reading, so storage is scaled to the basin's reported percent of median. A fair guess, flagged as one.
  • schematic The map geometry and the flow-and-mixing model — a deliberately simplified diagram of where water goes, not survey data.

Column dictionary

  • capacity_af / storage_af — total and current storage in acre-feet. An acre-foot floods one acre a foot deep: roughly what two Front Range households use in a year.
  • pct_of_median — storage against this station's median for this calendar week, 100 = a normal year. When live, it is derived from the reading and the baked median; otherwise it carries the operator's or basin's reported figure.
  • confidence / method — observed / measured: a published, dated reading; live / derived: DWR telemetry fetched by your browser, compared to the baked median; basin estimate / estimated: scaled from the basin's percent of median.
  • role — water supply, or flood control for the Corps of Engineers pools (Cherry Creek, Bear Creek): famous lakes nobody drinks from, whose capacity_af is just the small permanent pool and whose percent-of-median is blank because "normal storage" doesn't apply.
  • dwr_telemetry_abbrev — Colorado DWR station code, usable directly against the CDSS station tools and REST API.

Providers, systems and rights — what this does and doesn’t model

Three different things get called “your water,” and they are administered by three different bodies. This project models only the first, and loosely:

  • Who delivers it — the provider (or utility). A city department (Denver Water, Aurora Water), a special district (Parker Water & Sanitation), or a private company. This is the everyday sense and the one the story uses.
  • Who regulates its safety — the Public Water System (PWS). The unit defined by the federal Safe Drinking Water Act; each carries a PWSID, and in Colorado they are overseen by CDPHE. A provider is an organisation; a PWS is a regulated system, and one provider can operate more than one — so the two are not interchangeable.
  • Who is entitled to it — the water right. Administered separately by the Colorado Division of Water Resources under prior appropriation, across the seven water divisions that this site’s basins follow. A right governs diversion, not delivery or treatment.

The honest limits. Places here are grouped by provider and matched by ZIP prefix — and ZIP codes are postal routes, not service-area boundaries. One ZIP can span several providers, a provider spans many ZIPs, and about a third of our entries deliberately cover a cluster of small neighbouring systems under one label. We do not use PWSIDs or published service-area boundaries, so nothing here is authoritative for “who serves this address.”

For the authoritative answer, in order of usefulness: your utility bill; your provider’s own website; the EPA SDWIS federal reporting search (look up a system by state and name); and EPA’s explainer on public water systems. For plain-language background on Colorado water generally, Water Education Colorado is the standard reference.

How the snowpack-by-decade chart is built

A decade-to-decade comparison goes wrong the moment the measuring changes. SNOTEL sites have been added and retired steadily since 1978, and higher or snowier sites joining the network late would manufacture a trend that is really just a change in who is reporting. So that chart uses a fixed panel: only sites installed by 1980 that then reported in every water year since 1981 — currently 43 stations, 8,240–11,600 ft. The same 43 sites are averaged in every decade. A day is used only when at least 85% of the panel reports it, and a decade's curve is the median across that decade's water years, not a mean, so one freak season cannot carry a decade.

Two honest caveats. The 2020s bar covers only five completed water years, so it is the least settled figure on the chart. And a panel of long-record sites skews toward the mid-elevation band SNOTEL was built to watch — it is a consistent sample of Colorado's snowpack, not a complete census of it.

Where the elevations in the step-down come from

The basin step-down diagram stacks every reservoir and streamgage in order of how high it actually sits. Two sources feed that: for a gage, the USGS surveyed alt_va, the altitude of the gage datum; for a reservoir with DWR telemetry, the CDSS stage field — the live water-surface elevation, from the same reading that gives us the storage. That covers 25 of the reservoirs here.

For the rest there is no telemetered stage, so we try to read the water surface off the USGS 3DEP elevation model — but only accept it when the samples show a dominant flat surface, meaning we are genuinely looking at a lake. That threshold matters: the coordinates in our dataset are label anchors for the map, not survey points, and a few sit up to two kilometres off the water. A permissive version of this test put Homestake 1,160 feet too low by measuring a hillside, so the strict rule is deliberate — a confidently wrong height would mis-order the diagram while looking authoritative. Where the test passes, checked against the reservoirs CDSS does report a stage for, it lands within about 60 feet.

That gives a verified surface elevation for 34 of the 52 reservoirs, plus all 18 streamgages. The remaining reservoirs appear in the step-down with no elevation printed beside them — we will not show a number we cannot stand behind. They are still positioned, using the one thing the river guarantees: water runs downhill, so a reservoir sits below everything upstream of it and above everything downstream. Their placement is an inference from the river's own shape; every printed figure is a measurement.

Note that the two measured sources are not quite the same quantity. A CDSS stage is today's pool, which moves through the season; a 3DEP surface is where the water stood when the elevation model was flown. Both are the water surface, and the difference is far smaller than the gaps between reservoirs, which is all the ordering depends on. The vertical spacing in that diagram is deliberately not to scale — only the order is exact.

The two Lake Powell thresholds are elevations, converted to storage: minimum power pool (3,490 ft) from Reclamation's own daily record, which shows the reservoir holding 4.00 MAF at that elevation while filling in 1964, less the ~6.8% capacity since lost to sediment — the same record reads 5.91 MAF at 3,525 ft in 1965 against 5.51 MAF in 2026 — giving ≈3.7 MAF today. Dead pool (3,370 ft) is total capacity 25.16 MAF (USGS 2018 survey) less ~23.3 MAF of live capacity, ≈1.9 MAF.
