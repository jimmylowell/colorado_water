Where Colorado’s water comes from, where it is stored and how full it is, from the agencies’ own records.

  • Readings: Colorado DWR, USGS, USBR and NRCS (Data).
  • Not operational data. For decisions, go to the primary sources.
  • The reports, newest first, are on Reports: short dated check-ins through the year and the Water year 2026 report.

Water from over the mountains

  • The Colorado Water Plan puts about 70% of the state’s surface water west of the Divide, and about 80% if the Rio Grande basin is counted with the West Slope, the figure usually quoted.
  • About 80% of the state’s people live along the eastern slope (Wikipedia, Colorado–Big Thompson Project).
  • Tunnels under the Divide carry water east. Denver: the Moffat Tunnel and the 23.3-mile Roberts Tunnel from Dillon Reservoir (Wikipedia).
  • Fort Collins and the northern Front Range: Lake Granby water through the Adams Tunnel of the Colorado–Big Thompson Project.
  • Pueblo, Colorado Springs and the Arkansas Valley: the Fryingpan–Arkansas Project, into Turquoise Lake and Twin Lakes.
  • On the map the tunnels are the dashed ribbons crossing the dotted line; the flow view shows each city’s blend of sources.

Reading the glasses

  • Each reservoir is a glass, wide at the rim and narrow at the base. Its size is its capacity.
  • The level is storage, filled by area: a glass that looks half full holds half its capacity.
  • The color inside is the water’s source: each headwater has its own hue, and blends keep them traceable. Rivers use the same hues.
  • The bone-colored tick is the median level for that week.
  • A small ▼: storage fell over the past week.
  • On the flow view, selecting a reservoir or gage dims everything off its downstream path.
  • Cherry Creek and Bear Creek are drawn as flood-control pools, not supply. Cherry Creek Dam was built by the US Army Corps of Engineers for flood control (Wikipedia).

Methods

  • Committed data. The site renders from files in its repository, so it works offline. The reservoir and gage snapshot is dated 22 Jul 2026. The monthly history, weekly medians and Lake Powell series were rebuilt from the agencies’ records on 4 Nov 2026.
  • Daily readings. Once a day a job collects Colorado DWR CDSS telemetry (reservoir storage, and flow at the two DWR-operated gages) and USGS NWIS (flow at the other 16 gages). Visitors never query the agencies. A reading from that day is labelled live; an older one carries its date.
  • Observed and estimated. Solid-outlined glasses carry a published, dated reading (Denver Water, USBR, Northern Water, DWR). Dash-outlined glasses have none and show an estimate, labelled as one.
  • The month-by-month player is a reconstruction. Each basin’s monthly percent of median is derived from CDSS daily storage against each reservoir’s weekly median; the Yampa & White has no reporting reservoir, so its months are an estimate. Reservoirs are scaled by their basin’s figure. It shows the year’s shape, not a gage record.
  • Hydrographs are measured. A reservoir or gage in the data sheet draws about a year of daily CDSS storage or daily mean flow. The ▼ marker is the past week’s storage slope (1 acre-foot a day is about 0.5 cfs).
  • Flow composition is arithmetic. The flow view traces each river’s makeup edge by edge from the headwaters; diversions take proportional slices of typical late-July flows.
  • Braid, flow view only. Braid splits a river into its source shares, which needs the flow diagram’s tree of edges. On the map a river is one line with one blended color.
  • Every series is downloadable as plain CSV, with source and as-of columns per row.
